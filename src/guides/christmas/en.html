The holidays bring together people who rarely spend a whole evening in the same room: grandparents, teenagers, small children, in-laws. The right games turn that mix into the best part of the season. Here is how to choose them and a simple plan for Christmas Day and New Year's Eve.

From Francesco's experience

Every Christmas I prepare a game for the whole family. There are so many of us that I have to split everyone into three teams: things get loud quickly, but there is plenty of laughter.

What makes a good family holiday game

A game that works for an eight-year-old and an eighty-year-old at the same table has a few things in common:

  • Rules you can explain in one sentence. If it needs a rulebook, half the table will drift away.
  • Mixed teams. Put a child, a teenager and an adult on each team. Kids bring speed and pop culture, grandparents bring general knowledge, and nobody feels outmatched.
  • Knowledge from different generations. The best questions are the ones where the grandparents know the answer and the teenagers do not, and then the other way round.
  • Easy to pause. Someone will always need to check the oven or answer the door.

Christmas Day: games after lunch

After a big meal, energy is low and nobody wants to move. This is the moment for calm, seated games that still get people talking.

  • Sort It: each team puts a row of items in order, such as animals from fastest to slowest or inventions from oldest to newest. It is perfect for mixed ages, because younger players can drag the cards while older ones argue about the order.
  • Hidden Word Link: five words and forty seconds to find the one that links them all. A favourite with crossword lovers, and a good way to get quiet relatives involved.
  • Guess the Top 10: teams take turns naming items from a hidden top ten. With a big family, it creates loud debates about who said what first.

Tip: let the youngest player be the "host" who reads the questions and reveals the answers. Children love the role, and it keeps them at the table longer.

The evenings in between

The days between Christmas and New Year are ideal for longer game sessions with cousins and friends. Try a family championship: keep the same teams across several evenings and add up the scores. New Year's Eve: building up to midnight

New Year's Eve has a natural structure: dinner, a long wait and a countdown. Use games to fill the hours between dinner and midnight, getting faster and louder as midnight approaches.

Prizes and forfeits for the whole family

Small prizes make the competition feel special: the last chocolate in the box, choosing the film for the next evening, or the right to skip washing up. Keep forfeits gentle and age-appropriate: telling a joke, doing an impression, singing the chorus of a song. Avoid anything that could embarrass older relatives or upset younger children.

I often use these games as a qualifying round: right from the start I announce that the team with the most points will get an advantage in the treasure hunt I organise straight afterwards. And at the end of the treasure hunt there is always a sweet treat as the prize.

Practical tips for a smooth evening

  • Cast the game to the TV so grandparents do not have to read small text on a phone.
  • Turn up the font size on the device you are using if the TV is far away.
  • Keep rounds short when young children are playing: ten minutes is plenty before a change of game.
  • Have a plan B for when the youngest get tired: a game the adults can continue on their own.

Frequently asked questions

What games can grandparents and children play together?

Games with simple rules and mixed teams work best: sorting games, guessing games and team quizzes where different generations know different answers.

How can we fill the time until midnight on New Year's Eve?

Plan two or three team games that get faster as the evening goes on, and end with a final round that crowns the champions just before the countdown.

Do we need a board game or cards?

No. The games in this guide run in the browser on a phone, tablet, computer or smart TV, free and with no sign-up.
