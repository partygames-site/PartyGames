A children's birthday party lives or dies in the moments between cake and pick-up. Planned games keep a room full of excited kids happy and stop the chaos from taking over. This guide, written for parents, covers games by age, a ready-made 90-minute plan and how to handle the inevitable tears when someone loses.

Golden rules for any age

  • Alternate active and calm games. Running games burn energy; seated games bring it back down. Ending on a calm game makes pick-up much easier.
  • Keep each game short. Ten minutes is a long time for a six-year-old. Stop a game while kids still want more.
  • Everyone gets a small prize. A sticker or a sweet for taking part avoids most meltdowns. Save a special prize for the birthday child to hand out, not to win.
  • Prefer teams to elimination. In elimination games the kids who go out first spend the rest of the game bored. Team games keep everyone in until the end.
  • Have one adult in charge of games and another in charge of food and coats.

Ages 4 to 6: simple, physical, no reading

At this age, children cannot read questions and have short attention spans. Choose games with movement and clear, simple rules:

  • Musical statues: dance while the music plays, freeze when it stops. Instead of eliminating anyone, give a sticker to the best statue each round.
  • Treasure hunt: hide small objects around one room and give picture clues instead of written ones.
  • Simon says: perfect for calming the group before cake.

Screen games are not really needed at this age, and that is fine: the best party for a five-year-old involves a lot of running around.

Ages 7 to 9: first quiz games and team challenges

Children can now read short texts, enjoy competing in teams and love being faster than the adults. A screen game becomes a great centrepiece:

  • Skill Test: a grid of symbols, colours and shapes, and one target to spot. No reading needed, just sharp eyes, and kids are often quicker than grown-ups.
  • Fire Down: a category on screen and five seconds to name something that fits. Choose easy categories like animals or colours and let the adult host read them aloud.
  • Relay races in the garden or hallway, if there is space, to burn off energy between screen games.

Tip: make the birthday child the host who presses the buttons and announces the scores. It gives them a special role without the pressure of having to win.

Ages 10 to 12: quizzes, music and real competition

Pre-teens want games that feel grown-up. Team quizzes, music and a proper scoreboard work very well:

  • Emoji Song Quiz: guess famous songs from a row of emoji. Pre-teens often know far more current songs than their parents.
  • Odd One Out: teams take turns flipping cards and must avoid the one that does not belong. It is tense, quick and easy to understand.
  • Let them name their own teams. On PartyGames Club you can set team names and colours on the home page, and they will appear in every team game.

How to deal with tears and arguments

  • Praise effort, not only results: "best team spirit" or "funniest answer" prizes spread the wins around.
  • Mix teams yourself, balancing ages and separating best friends who might gang up.
  • Move on quickly after a loss: starting the next game straight away is the best distraction.
  • Watch the screen time: one or two screen games are plenty in a 90-minute party.

Frequently asked questions

How many games do I need for a kids' birthday party?

For a 90-minute party, plan four or five short games, alternating active and calm ones, plus one spare in case a game does not work.

Are online party games suitable for children?

From about seven, yes, as long as an adult hosts the game and the content is age-appropriate. Games based on observation, like Skill Test, need no reading at all.

How do I stop kids from getting upset when they lose?

Use team games instead of elimination games, give everyone a small prize for taking part and start the next game quickly after each round.
