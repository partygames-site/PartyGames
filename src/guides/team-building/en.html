Most people groan when they hear "team building". That is usually because the activities are too long, too personal or too obviously forced. The games that work at work are short, inclusive and genuinely fun, and they need no trust falls. Here is how to run them in the office, at an offsite or on a video call.

From Francesco's experience

I work in IT, and the best team building I have experienced did not happen in a meeting room but over dinners with colleagues, who became friends outside the office too. During coffee breaks I often read out one of my quizzes: it always ends with a laugh or some friendly teasing.

What makes a work game succeed

  • It is short. Fifteen to thirty minutes at the start or end of a meeting is plenty. Nobody wants to lose an afternoon.
  • It is opt-in and low pressure. Nobody should be forced to perform, share personal stories or be singled out.
  • It mixes people. The real value is getting people who rarely work together onto the same team.
  • Skills are varied. Mix questions on logic, general knowledge, numbers and words, so different people get to shine.
  • It works for everyone. Avoid games that depend on physical ability, a particular culture or one language level.

Quick games for the start of a meeting (10–15 minutes)

A short game at the start of a weekly meeting wakes people up and makes the meeting itself more relaxed.

  • Hidden Word Link: five clues, one hidden word, forty seconds. Split the meeting into two or three teams and play three rounds. It takes ten minutes and gets everyone thinking.
  • Sort It: teams put items in order, such as countries by population or inventions by year. It sparks quick debates without anyone feeling exposed.

Longer sessions for offsites and team days (30–60 minutes)

When you have more time, a tournament with a running scoreboard gives the session a shape and a winner.

  • Lightning Duel: riddles, logic, sequences, mental maths, anagrams and memory challenges, each worth one point. The variety means that the finance team, the designers and the developers each get their moment.
  • Stack Up: teams estimate values such as populations, heights or distances. Estimation games are great at work because they reward reasoning and discussion rather than trivia knowledge.
  • Guess the Top 10: a hidden ranking to fill in turn by turn. It works well as the final round of a tournament.

Tip: appoint a neutral host, ideally someone who does not usually lead meetings. It shifts the usual hierarchy and makes the session feel less like work.

Running games with remote and hybrid teams

Online games work surprisingly well for distributed teams, as long as you plan around the video call:

  1. The host shares their screen with the game open in the browser. Players do not need to install anything.
  2. Use breakout rooms for team discussions, then bring everyone back to give the answer.
  3. Choose games with clear visual questions, because audio quality varies on calls.
  4. Keep it under 30 minutes. Video call fatigue sets in faster than in person.
  5. In hybrid meetings, make sure each team has both office and remote members, so the remote people are not just spectators.

Things to avoid

  • Personal questions. Games that dig into private life can make colleagues uncomfortable. Keep it to general knowledge, logic and light humour.
  • Forced forfeits. Penalties that are fun among friends can feel humiliating at work.
  • Individual rankings. Team scores are fine; publicly ranking individuals is not.
  • Making it mandatory after hours. Run games during working time, or make clear that joining is optional.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a team building game last?

Ten to fifteen minutes at the start of a meeting, or thirty to sixty minutes for a dedicated session. Shorter is almost always better.

Can these games be played on a video call?

Yes. The host shares their screen with the game open in a browser, teams discuss in breakout rooms and come back to answer. Nobody needs to install anything.

What kind of team building games are best for work?

Short team quizzes, logic puzzles and estimation games. They mix people, reward different skills and avoid personal or physical challenges.
