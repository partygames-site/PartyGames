A good game night is not about owning the most games. It is about getting the right people around the table, keeping the energy up and knowing when to switch to the next game. This guide walks you through the whole evening, from the invitations to the last round.

From Francesco's experience

I inherited my love of organising games from my mum, who used to set up treasure hunts for my brother and me when we were kids. Adult life gets more complicated, so I asked myself: why not have just as much fun as grown-ups, with similar games made harder?

When it rains or nobody knows what to do, I host a game night at home with friends. I act as the game master: I connect the laptop to the TV, form balanced teams of two to four people (mixing those who don't know each other, so new friendships can start), pick the games and keep the score. The real skill of a game master is managing the room and settling disputes, for example when two teams both think they deserve the point. There is one basic rule: the game master decides.

1. Invite the right number of people

The ideal game night has six to ten players. With fewer than five, team games lose their buzz; with more than twelve, people start waiting too long for their turn and side conversations take over. If you expect a bigger crowd, plan for games that split everyone into two or three teams, so nobody is ever just watching.

Mix people who already know each other with one or two newcomers, but avoid a room where only the host knows everyone. If that is your situation, start with the games in our icebreaker guide before moving on to anything competitive.

2. Plan three or four games, not ten

The most common mistake is preparing too many games. In a typical three-hour evening you will realistically play three or four, with a break in the middle. Choose them so that the evening has a shape:

  1. A warm-up (10–15 minutes). Something with no rules to explain and no wrong answers, so late arrivals can join mid-round. Most Likely To is perfect: everyone votes, everyone laughs, nobody can lose badly.
  2. The main event (30–45 minutes). A team game with scores, where the competition builds. Guess the Top 10 or Emoji Song Quiz work well because the whole team discusses every answer.
  3. A fast palate cleanser (10 minutes). After a long game, switch to something quick and loud like Fire Down, where you have five seconds to answer.
  4. A closer. End on a game that leaves people talking, ideally one that is easy to stop at any moment, because energy drops fast after 11 p.m.

Tip: always know which game comes next. The dead minutes spent deciding what to play are where game nights lose momentum.

3. Prepare the space

You do not need a big table. What matters is that everyone can see the same screen and hear each other:

  • One shared screen. Open the game on a laptop or tablet and, if you can, cast it to the TV so the whole room can read the questions. A phone works for small groups of up to five.
  • Seats in a circle or a U shape, facing the screen. Teams should sit together so they can whisper.
  • Pen and paper for games where teams write their answers, plus a few spare pens because they always disappear.
  • Lighting: bright enough to read, warm enough to feel like an evening at home rather than a meeting room.

4. Make teams quickly and fairly

Picking teams can take ten awkward minutes if you let it. Decide them yourself before the main game and mix them up: split couples, split the two most competitive friends, and put the quieter guests with people who will include them. On PartyGames Club you can set team names, colours and scores once on the home page and every team game uses them automatically, so the leaderboard carries over from one game to the next.

Two teams are the easiest to manage. With more than ten players, three teams of three or four keep everyone involved, because smaller teams mean everyone has to contribute.

5. Explain rules in under a minute

Nobody wants a rules lecture. Read the rules yourself in advance, then explain only what people need for the first round: the goal, how you score and what happens on your turn. Play a practice round with no points. Edge cases can be explained when they come up. Every game on this site has a "Rules and tips" section below the game area if someone wants the details.

Before every game I ask for quiet, explain the rules and play a practice match as an example. When I notice that the same people always answer, I cheer on the quieter ones, switch to a game on a topic they know well (music, films…), or choose a game where only one person per team can answer and let the shyest players take the turn.

6. Food that works with games

Choose snacks that can be eaten with one hand and do not leave greasy fingers on phones and pens: popcorn, crackers, cut vegetables with dips, mini sandwiches, fruit. Put them in several small bowls around the room instead of one big tray, so nobody has to leave their seat. Serve anything that needs plates during the break between the main event and the fast game.

7. Keep the energy up

  • Watch the room, not the scoreboard. If people are checking their phones, cut the round short and switch games.
  • Rotate the host role. Let a different player read the questions each round, so you get to play too.
  • Keep forfeits light. Silly challenges like speaking in rhyme or imitating an animal are fun; anything embarrassing or involving food or money is not.
  • Celebrate the winners briefly and move on. Long victory laps kill the mood for the losing team.

Frequently asked questions

How many games should I prepare for a game night?

Three or four for a three-hour evening, plus one spare in case a game does not land. Preparing more usually means spending time choosing instead of playing.

What if some guests do not like competitive games?

Open with games that have no winners, like voting or debate games, and keep scores light-hearted. Team games also help, because nobody is singled out when the team loses.

Do we need to buy anything?

No. All the games mentioned here are free and run in the browser on a phone, tablet, computer or smart TV, with no app and no sign-up. Pen and paper are useful for a couple of them.

How long should a game night last?

Two and a half to three hours is the sweet spot. It is better to end while people are still having fun than to push on until everyone is tired.
